01 — What the research says

Five findings that decide the design

I looked at what the incumbent tools actually do, what applicant tracking systems actually reject, and which data sources are actually available without a legal grey area. Five things came back that change what this app should be.

01

Finding jobs is solved. Tailoring is the bottleneck.

Between one aggregator API and the free per-company ATS feeds, you can have more relevant listings than you can possibly action. Adzuna alone gives 1,000 calls a month free across UK/US/EU; Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby each expose public JSON for every company on their platform. Nobody is short of listings. What costs you is the 45–90 minutes per application spent rereading the job description, rewriting six bullets, drafting a letter, and retyping the same answers into a form.

Design consequenceThe primary object in the app is an application, not a listing. The job feed is an input to it, not the product.
02

The ATS is not the thing rejecting you.

The folk model — a robot scores your CV and bins it — is mostly wrong in 2026. Workday, iCIMS, Greenhouse and Lever all parse ordinary formatted PDFs correctly, provided you avoid tables, text boxes, multi-column layouts, and content stuck in headers or footers. The fast rejections come overwhelmingly from two places: knockout screening questions (visa status, salary, notice period, years-with-X) and parse failures that leave your record half-empty in the database. After that, a human recruiter runs keyword filters over the database to decide who to read.

Design consequenceScreening questions get first-class treatment — every incumbent treats them as an afterthought. And the CV renderer is deliberately structurally boring: one column, no tables, real text.
03

Keyword stuffing is now a penalty, not a hack.

White text and hidden keywords have been detected and flagged since roughly 2016. Semantic matchers score relevance in context, so repeating a term lowers your score rather than raising it. The single highest-leverage edit is much duller: aligning your headline job title with the posting's job title. (Vendor research claims a very large effect here; treat the magnitude with suspicion, but the direction is well supported.)

Design consequenceThe match score is evidence-based, not term-frequency-based. The question it answers is "can he prove this requirement?" — not "does this word appear?"
04

The incumbents split one workflow across three products.

Jobscan owns ATS match scoring. Teal owns the kanban tracker with tailoring bolted on. Simplify owns autofill. Between $0 and about $59 a month, three logins, and three separate half-copies of your career history. None of them holds a single authoritative record of what you've actually done, so none can reason well about any individual job.

Design consequenceOne master profile is the whole edge. Matching, tailoring, letter-writing and form-filling all read from the same store, so improving it improves everything downstream at once.
05

The failure mode of AI CV tools is fabrication.

Point a language model at a job description and ask it to make a CV match, and it will helpfully invent the missing experience — a plausible metric, a technology you touched once, a scope you didn't have. It reads beautifully and it ends the interview the moment someone probes it. This is the risk that matters, and it's structural: the model is being asked to close a gap it has no evidence for.

Design consequenceGeneration is bound to evidence. This is the architectural spine of the whole app — see below.
02 — The spine

Evidence-bound generation

One rule the architecture enforces rather than requests: every generated line carries the ID of the fact it came from.

You maintain an evidence bank — atomic, dated, quantified records of things you actually did. "Cut deployment time from 40 min to 6 min by moving CI to self-hosted runners, 2024, team of 4." Each one is a card with tags, a date, a scope, and a number where one exists.

When the app writes a CV bullet or a paragraph of a cover letter, it may only rephrase, compress, and reframe cards from that bank — and the draft shows you which card each line came from. If a requirement has no matching card, the app is forbidden from writing to it. It surfaces the hole as a gap you can see, and offers you three honest moves: add a card if you do in fact have the experience, address the gap directly in the letter, or accept it and apply anyway.

Mechanically this is a strict structured output: the model returns { text, evidence_ids[] } per bullet, and a validation pass rejects any bullet whose evidence array is empty or references a card whose content doesn't support it. Anything that fails goes back as a gap, never into the document. That check is cheap, and it is the difference between a tool you can send from and a tool that eventually embarrasses you.

03 — The core loop

Five steps, one screen each

STEP 1

Capture

Jobs arrive from the aggregator, from company ATS feeds you follow, from a URL you paste, or from the extension as you browse.

STEP 2

Screen

A cheap model extracts the requirements and scores each against your evidence bank. Covered, partial, or gap — per requirement.

STEP 3

Tailor

You pick one. The app proposes bullet rewrites, each anchored to a card. Gaps stay visible as gaps.

STEP 4

Pack

CV, cover letter and pre-written screening answers on one page, each with a copy button and an export.

STEP 5

Track

Status, dates, follow-ups. Outcomes feed back so you learn which evidence actually earns interviews.

04 — The screens

Four views

Designed as a dense working tool, not a marketing surface. Everything that needs your attention encodes its state in shape and colour as well as number, so the whole list reads at a glance.

05 — Data model

Five tables, one of which is the product

The profile follows the JSON Resume schema so you can render with any of its hundreds of themes and import from tools that already speak it. The evidence bank is the extension that makes the rest work — it has no equivalent in JSON Resume, and it is where your time goes.

// Profile — JSON Resume 1.0.0 shape, stored as JSON. The renderable facts. Profile basics · work[] · education[] · skills[] · projects[] + constants: visa status, notice period, salary band, locations, demographics, referral source, links // Evidence — the bank. Atomic, dated, quantified. THIS is the product. Evidence id EV-014 claim "Migrated 60-service estate to EKS, zero downtime" metric { value: 34, unit: "%", of: "infra spend", direction: "down" } period 2023-04 → 2023-11 scope "lead, 4 engineers" tags [kubernetes, aws, migration, cost] employerId → Profile.work[] strength owned | contributed | exposed ← drives covered/partial/gap // Job — normalised across every source adapter. Job source adzuna | greenhouse | lever | ashby | manual | extension sourceRef · url · title · company · location · remote · salary descriptionRaw · requirements[] ← extracted, not scraped text postedAt · closesAt · dedupeHash // Application — the working object. One per job you act on. Application jobId · status (drafted|applied|screening|interviewing|closed) matchScore · coverage[] { requirementId, verdict, evidenceIds[] } tailoredBullets[] { text, evidenceIds[], baseBulletId } coverLetter · answers[] renderedCvPath · appliedAt · outcome · outcomeNote // Answer — the reusable bank of screening-question responses. Answer questionHash · canonicalQuestion · text · evidenceIds[] timesUsed · lastUsedAt ← fuzzy-matched against new forms

Why strength earns its column: it's what separates a real match from a keyword collision. "Used Terraform on one project in 2022" is exposed; "owned the Terraform estate for two years" is owned. The same tag, three very different answers to the same requirement — and the difference between an honest partial and an overclaim.

06 — Architecture

How it fits together

Next.js (App Router) · TypeScript · Prisma ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ UI Shortlist · Tailor · Pack · Pipeline · Evidence · Profile │ └───────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────┘ │ server actions / route handlers ┌───────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────┐ │ Core │ │ ingest adapter → normalise → dedupe → store │ │ extract job description → requirements[] (Haiku, batch) │ │ match requirements × evidence → coverage[] │ │ tailor coverage → bullets{text, evidenceIds} (Opus 5) │ │ validate reject any claim not supported by cited evidence │ │ render Profile + bullets → PDF / DOCX / plain text │ └────┬──────────────────────┬───────────────────────┬──────────────┘ │ │ │ ┌────┴─────────┐ ┌────────┴────────┐ ┌──────────┴───────────┐ │ Sources │ │ Anthropic SDK │ │ Prisma → SQLite │ │ adzuna │ │ opus 5 · haiku │ │ (→ Postgres later) │ │ greenhouse │ │ structured out │ │ │ │ lever │ │ prompt caching │ │ evidence bank │ │ ashby │ │ batch API │ │ is the crown jewel │ │ manual-url │ └─────────────────┘ └──────────────────────┘ │ extension │ └──────────────┘

Source adapters are one interface

Every source implements fetch() → NormalisedJob[] and nothing else knows the difference. Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby are public JSON endpoints their own careers pages already call — no keys, no bot walls, no breakage when a careers page is redesigned. Adzuna needs an app ID and key, gives 1,000 calls a month free, and returns truncated descriptions, so the adapter follows through to the posting URL when a job scores well enough to be worth the full text.

Rendering the CV

The document renderer takes the profile plus the tailored bullets and emits a single-column PDF with real text, no tables, no text boxes, and nothing in the header or footer — precisely the shape finding 02 says parses cleanly. Headless Chromium against an HTML template is the pragmatic route: you get exact typographic control and DOCX from the same template via a second exporter. Keep the template in the repo; it's the one piece of the app you'll fiddle with forever.

Multi-tenant-ready without being multi-tenant

Per your scope answer: every table carries a userId from day one and every query goes through a repository layer that scopes by it, but there is exactly one user row and no login screen. API keys live server-side in environment variables, never in the client. That is roughly a day of extra discipline in phase 1, and it's the difference between adding auth later and rewriting the data layer later.

07 — The model layer

Which model does what, and what it costs

Three techniques do most of the work here. Structured outputs with strict schemas, so extraction and tailoring return validated objects rather than prose you have to parse. Prompt caching, because your profile and evidence bank are a large, byte-identical prefix on every single request — cache it once and the marginal cost of scoring another job collapses. And the Batch API at half price for the overnight scoring run, which has no latency requirement at all.

Overnight match scoringHaiku 4.5, batchedHundreds of jobs, no user waiting. Batch halves it; the cached evidence-bank prefix does the rest.$1 · $5 −50%
Bullet tailoringOpus 5The judgement call — what to foreground, what to compress, what not to claim. This is where quality is visible and where you should not economise.$5 · $25
Cover letter + screening answersOpus 5Prose you'll actually send under your own name. Adaptive thinking on; effort high.$5 · $25
Evidence validationHaiku 4.5Narrow entailment check: does card EV-014 support this sentence? Cheap, and the last line before a claim reaches a document.$1 · $5

What that adds up to. A full tailoring run — extraction, matching, six bullets, a letter, six answers, validation — lands around $0.15–0.30. Overnight scoring of a couple of hundred new listings is cents once the prefix is cached. At twenty applications a week you're looking at roughly $5–15 a month, which is under the cheapest incumbent's single-product subscription and buys you all three functions.

08 — Build plan

Four phases, each independently useful

The ordering principle: the thing that saves you the most time per hour of build is the paste-a-URL path. It needs no source integrations at all, and it is the entire value proposition. Everything else is convenience layered on top.

PHASE 0

Seed the evidence bank

Half a day · mostly your time, not build time
  • Parse your existing CV into candidate evidence cards
  • An editor screen where you correct, split, date, and quantify each one
  • Set the constants: visa, notice, salary band, locations, links
Done whenYou have 25–40 real cards with numbers on them. Nothing downstream works properly without this, and no amount of clever prompting substitutes for it.
PHASE 1

The single-application loop

About a week · this is the release that changes your life
  • Paste a URL or a job description → extract requirements
  • Coverage report against the evidence bank
  • Tailored bullets with evidence binding, plus the validator
  • Cover letter and screening answers
  • PDF export from the HTML template
  • SQLite, no auth, userId everywhere
Done whenYou can go from a job URL to a sendable pack in under ten minutes, and the unsupported-claims counter reads zero.
PHASE 2

Sources and pipeline

About a week
  • Adapters: Adzuna, plus Greenhouse/Lever/Ashby for a company list you curate
  • Dedupe across sources — the same role arrives three times
  • Shortlist screen with overnight batch scoring
  • Pipeline board, follow-up nudges, outcome recording
  • DOCX export
Done whenYou open the app in the morning to a scored shortlist you didn't assemble by hand.
PHASE 3

Browser extension

1–2 weeks · the maintenance-heavy phase
  • Capture: one click sends the listing you're viewing — including LinkedIn and Indeed pages you found yourself — into Dossier
  • Autofill: Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby first; their DOM is clean and stable
  • Workday last, behind a fallback to copy-paste — its custom SPA components have no conventional field names and break routinely
  • Always fills, never submits. You review every field
Done whenA Greenhouse application takes under two minutes end to end. Budget ongoing time for selector rot — this phase is never "finished".
PHASE 4

Productise — only if you decide to

Not scheduled
  • Auth, Postgres, per-user encryption of CV content at rest
  • Billing, usage caps, model-cost accounting per user
  • A lawful basis and a data-processing story: other people's CVs are personal data, and some of it is special-category
NotePhases 0–3 are yours to build however you like. Phase 4 changes the legal character of the project, and it's worth deciding deliberately rather than drifting into it.
09 — Risks

What could go wrong

LinkedIn and Indeed scraping

You asked for it, so it's in the plan — but implemented as extension capture rather than server-side scraping. When you're logged in and looking at a listing, the extension reads the page you're already viewing and sends it to Dossier. That gets you the same coverage without an IP that gets blocked within a week, and without a crawler hitting their servers. Server-side scraping of both sites is against their terms, aggressively defended, and carries account-ban risk. The capture path is my recommendation; if you want the crawler anyway, it slots in behind the same adapter interface.

Fabrication leaks past the validator

The evidence binding makes fabrication hard, not impossible — a model can cite a real card and still overstate what it says. Mitigation is layered: strict schemas, the entailment check, the unsupported-claims counter surfaced on the pack screen, and a hard rule that you read the letter before sending. Treat any leak you spot as a bug with a regression test, not a one-off.

Extension selector rot

ATS vendors redesign their forms and your selectors die silently — filling the wrong field is worse than filling nothing. Build it to detect its own failure: if the expected field count doesn't match, refuse to fill and say so rather than guessing.

Truncated aggregator descriptions

Adzuna returns snippets, and requirement extraction on a snippet produces a confidently wrong coverage report. The adapter must mark jobs as partial-text and either fetch the full posting or refuse to score them above a provisional band.

Volume becomes the goal

Any tool that makes applying cheap tempts you to apply more rather than better, which is exactly the behaviour that gets ignored. Worth designing against: the pipeline board tracks response rate, not application count, and the shortlist defaults to hiding anything under a 60 match.
